Summary

Due to the pace of recent climate change, many trees and the vegetation types they characterize find themselves outside the climate conditions to which they are best adapted. The rate, magnitude, and impacts of expected vegetation shifts are still largely unknown, but there is broad expectation that many trees are unlikely to keep pace with the rate of contemporary environmental change. These vegetation transitions will shape the future of the terrestrial biosphere.

My research focuses on vegetation transitions induced by climate change. I draw on expertise in natural history, large-scale geospatial modeling, community science, and environmental ethics to address:

  1. Where and how vegetation-type transitions are occurring.
  2. What the impacts of these transitions are on local ecosystems and human communities.
  3. How to manage these transitions in ethical and effective ways.

To ethically and effectively manage vegetation transitions—and global change more generally—we must build durable and reciprocal relationships between analytical scientists, decision-makers, and community members (Fig. 1). Community science (also called “citizen” or “participatory” science) and the immense amount of biodiversity data it generates provide a powerful medium for developing and sustaining these relationships. A key feature of my work is to strengthen and integrate the relationships that connect analytical scientists studying ecosystem change with the decision-makers and community members who manage and are affected by those ecosystems.

Past Research

My research trajectory has been to accrete and integrate different disciplines necessary for a comprehensive understanding of modern forest biogeography. As a botany undergraduate in the Cornell Department of Plant Biology, I performed field and analytical work to map the altitude limits of five subalpine conifer species in the Sierra Nevada mountains and used these data to improve species distribution models. Under the mentorship of Kevin Nixon I wrote an iOS app for data collection and spent two months in the field doing altitude transects throughout subalpine Sierra Nevada forests. This work, which culminated in an honors thesis, provides a foundation in natural history that has informed all subsequent work.

At the beginning of my PhD I oriented my research to incorporate data science, climate modeling, and fire ecology to understand vegetation change at continental scales. This shift in technique and focus occurred for two reasons: (1) I realize that in order to understand modern plant distributions I also needed to understand anthropogenic activity and its impacts, and (2) a 2017 wildfire in Napa County burned through the forest I grew up in and deeply affected my family and my subsequent motivations.

Under the mentorship of Chris Field, I focused on furthering our understanding of changing vegetation (Fig. 1, line 1)—specifically the rate at which trees respond to warming and drying conditions in California and the role of wildfire in these transitions. Using 74,000 USFS Forest Inventory Analysis plots, I built climatic niche models for the seedlings and mature trees of 26 species across the western U.S. and found that 8 species appeared to shift their distribution to cooler and wetter climates and, of those, 2 species (Pseudotsuga menziesii and Quercus chrysolepis) seemed able to shift their distributions more quickly where wildfire had recently occurred (Hill & Field 2021, Nature Communications). I had identified a relationship between climate-induced vegetation transitions and wildfire. Following the dramatic increase in wildfires in CA since 2017 and the expected impacts on vegetation redistribution our research suggested, my next goal was to find an ecosystem to drill down into the characteristics, impacts, and management opportunities of vegetation transitions.

I focused on the low-elevation conifer forests of the Sierra Nevada, where the lower range limit is largely determined by warmer temperatures and lower precipitation. Using historical vegetation maps from the Wieslander Survey, I modeled the climate conditions that best described the 1930s distribution of conifers in the Sierras and then estimated where those conditions are today. I found that up to 20% of conifer forests in the Sierras today fall outside of the climate conditions that they occurred in 80 years ago (Hill et al. 2023, PNAS Nexus). These first-of-their-kind maps of vegetation-climate mismatch (VCM) provide a unique and potentially valuable case study to investigate the mechanics, impacts, and management options for ongoing vegetation transitions in nearly real-time. Additionally, this research has proved to be of wide public interest (being featured in stories by the NY Times, LA Times, and NPR) and has served as an effective platform to communicate the impacts of climate change on terrestrial ecosystems, especially after we coined the term “zombie forests” to refer to them in public outreach). This experience galvanized my commitment to communicating the insights of analytical science to communities and decision-makers (Fig. 1, lines 5 & 6). I am continuing this work as PI on a 3-year grant from the Moore Foundation.

These maps of vegetation-climate mismatch in the Sierra Nevada suggest profound implications for forest management, including carbon sequestration, wildfire regime, timber harvest, and recreation. My primary interest in management tended toward environmental ethics as I grappled with the ongoing controversy around assisted migration. I began investigating the waning utility of 'native' in areas where environments rapidly change and can no longer support the historical assemblage of species, such as forests experiencing VCM (Hill & Hadly 2018, Frontiers in Earth Science). This work developed into an effort to re-conceptualize nativeness as a gradient to allow for the attribution of moral value to species that shift their distributions in response to climate change (Hill 2024, Journal of Agriculture and Environmental Ethics). In the context of VCM, this novel ethical framework permits the management of vegetation transitions when the resulting assemblages provide greater net positive value—across biodiversity, ecosystem services, and community well-being.

The ethics of managing vegetation and ecosystem transitions underscores the importance of public engagement––the public provides many of the values that (should) go into management decisions (Fig. 1, line 9). Towards the end of my PhD and throughout my Post Doc, I increasingly emphasized public engagement, using community science (also known as 'participatory' or 'citizen' science) as a vehicle to both gather data and to engage broad audiences. In my time as a postdoc at the California Academy of Sciences I helped lead a number of community science events, including bio-blitzes (a biodiversity field survey with volunteer community scientists) and community conversations about the utility and life cycle of community-collected biodiversity data. In July of 2024 and May of 2025 I organized and led a bio-blitz in a Sierra Nevada ponderosa pine forest to (1) collect data on understory species in forests experiencing VCM and (2) engage the locals of Grass Valley, CA with research on the vegetation transitions occurring in their community.

I believe it is essential to cultivate relationships with community members and decision-makers that encompass more than data production. At the California Academy of Sciences I have given a number of public talks on global change ecology and  co-developed an exhibit on the main museum floor relating to VCM. I am committed to engaging Indigenous communities and traditional ecological knowledge in my work. I have participated in cultural burns and helped organize a convening between scientists at the California Academy of Sciences and native gatherers from across California to discuss research opportunities aimed at helping native gatherers mitigate the loss of resources and medicine. My recent policy-relevant work includes co-authorship on the "Nature and Climate Change" chapter in the inaugural United By Nature report (formerly the National Nature Assessment) and the analysis and visualization of urban habitats of conservation-target species in the SF Bay Area to inform regional conservation priorities (via the Conservation Lands Network).

An increasingly important undercurrent of my research—which I believe strengthens nearly all connections in the life cycle of community science data—is a commitment to furthering data accessibility through advanced geospatial technology. I recently worked with the California Native Plant Society to turn the results from their Fire Followers iNaturalist campaign into a data dashboard for decision-makers and community scientists alike. My data analysis workflows increasingly incorporate cloud-native data sources, and I am contributing to the development of open source tools like the mapgl R package. Additionally, Large Language Models (LLMs) are driving change in many industries and have great potential to improve biodiversity data accessibility. I’m co-I on a NASA ROSES grant to build LLM-integrated decision-support tools for conservation practitioners, allowing them to use natural language queries to interact with complex biodiversity data.

Future directions

My lab will investigate the behavior and management of changing ecosystems in general, and vegetation transitions in particular. This work will be organized around three guiding questions: (1) Where are vegetation-type transitions occurring or expected? (2) What are the impacts of these transitions on local ecosystems (including humans) (3) How do we manage these transitions in ethical and effective ways?

Ultimately, my research group will lead the field in understanding the geography, impacts, and management of vegetation-climate mismatch in western forests. Using VCM as our case study, we will make broad contributions to global change ecology by bringing innovative technology to biodiversity monitoring and by strengthening reciprocal information flows between community members, scientists, and decision-makers. We will develop accessible, dynamic platforms for vegetation-change monitoring that integrate community science, remote sensing, and natural-history collections to deliver generalizable insights and galvanize ethical, effective management.

I believe that ecological research must be grounded in specific localities in order to be impactful and generalizable. My research will first focus on the low-elevation conifer forests of the Sierra Nevada, with a framework designed to scale broadly. My students and I will use field work and modeling approaches (e.g. habitat suitability modeling) to identify ongoing and expected transitions, and machine learning techniques to identify generalizable signals of these transitions using point-occurrence and remote sensing data. We will investigate the impacts of vegetation turnover on various ecosystem services, with a particular focus on wildfire regimes and their attendant impacts.

Building on the July 2024 and 2025 bio-blitzes in Grass Valley, I will expand this work into a coordinated, Sierra-wide community-science campaign focused on faster-moving understory indicators of vegetation-climate mismatch and future transitions, supported by interactive, iterative data dashboards that show participants how their observations contribute to analysis and management. We will also build relationships with community members and decision-makers to develop effective management plans, which may include forest thinning, cultural burning, or assisted migration. In parallel, we will continue developing cloud-native platforms and tooling that strengthen the reciprocity and magnitude of connections in the life cycle of community-science data (Fig. 1): because iNaturalist and GBIF occurrence data are available in near real-time, we can shift from opportunistic sampling (e.g., the City Nature Challenge—the largest community science campaign in the world) to iterative, adaptive campaigns, using prioritization tools that update as new data arrive and management needs evolve.

Conclusion

Vegetation transitions will shape the future of the terrestrial biosphere. My research draws from natural history, data science, community science, and ethics, to bring a combination of analytical power and community-engaged solution options to modern forest biogeography. My ability and commitment to not only understand ecological change, but to strengthen reciprocal relationships with community members and decision-makers—via data production, modeling, data accessibility, and community outreach—positions me to advance modern vegetation change research and its application to ethical, effective management.
